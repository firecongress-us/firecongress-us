Rep. Tim Walberg (R-MI) purchased up to $560,000 in fossil fuel and defense contractor stocks on February 7, 2025, while serving on the House Committee on Natural Resources, the committee with jurisdiction over petroleum extraction and conservation on federal public lands. He then failed to disclose those purchases for more than 14 months, violating the federal disclosure law that applies to every member of Congress. The filing he made in early June 2026 was the first stock disclosure he had made in 15 years of congressional service.

## The Disclosure Failure

The Stop Trading on Congressional Knowledge (STOCK) Act requires members of Congress to report stock transactions within 45 days of making them. Walberg purchased shares in eight companies on February 7, 2025, but the required disclosure was not filed until June 3, 2026, according to reporting by NOTUS. That gap represents more than 14 months past the legal deadline.

The purchases disclosed include:

- Chevron (CVX): between $15,000 and $50,000
- Exxon Mobil (XOM): between $15,000 and $50,000
- Boeing (BA): between $15,000 and $50,000
- L3Harris Technologies (LHX): between $15,000 and $50,000
- Lockheed Martin (LMT): between $1,000 and $15,000
- Apple (AAPL): between $15,000 and $50,000
- Amazon (AMZN): between $1,000 and $15,000
- Jacobs Solutions (J): between $15,000 and $50,000

The combined value of the portfolio ranges from approximately $154,014 to $560,000, based on the disclosure ranges reported by NOTUS.

Walberg has served in the House since 2011. The June 2026 filing represents the first stock disclosure he has ever made in his congressional career, according to Benzinga's analysis of congressional trading records.

Walberg's spokesperson told NOTUS that a financial adviser managed the portfolio and executed the trades without notifying Walberg. The STOCK Act does not exempt members from the disclosure requirement based on how trades were executed; members remain personally responsible for timely disclosure regardless of who manages their accounts. The standard penalty for missing the 45-day disclosure deadline is a $200 fine.

## The Committee Conflict

At the time of the purchases, Walberg served on the House Committee on Natural Resources. That committee has jurisdiction over, among other things, petroleum conservation on public lands and the Trans-Alaska Oil Pipeline System. Two of the companies in Walberg's disclosed portfolio, Chevron and Exxon Mobil, are major producers of oil and natural gas on federal lands subject to Natural Resources Committee oversight.

The committee also addresses mining, energy resources on federal lands, and water and wildlife management, according to committee documentation. Walberg's subcommittee assignments in the 119th Congress include Indian and Insular Affairs and Water, Wildlife and Fisheries.

Additionally, three of the companies in his portfolio, Boeing, L3Harris Technologies, and Lockheed Martin, are among the largest U.S. defense contractors. While Walberg does not serve on the House Armed Services Committee, these companies lobby extensively on federal defense contracting and export policy.

## Voting Record

While holding these undisclosed stocks, Walberg's voting record on energy and environment reflected consistent support for fossil fuel industry positions.

The League of Conservation Voters (LCV), a nonprofit that scores members of Congress on their votes related to environment and clean energy, gave Walberg a score of 0% for 2025. That score means he cast no pro-environment votes among the measures the LCV tracked that year.

His 2025 anti-environment votes, as documented by the LCV, include:

- Rolling back the federal methane pollution fee
- Blocking cost-saving water heater efficiency standards
- Blocking state cleaner truck emissions standards
- Rolling back Clean Air Act protections
- Voting for the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which the LCV characterized as "Raising Energy Costs and Providing Polluter Giveaways to Cut Taxes for Billionaires"

Walberg's lifetime LCV score is 4%, reflecting a long-standing pattern of voting against environmental legislation across his career in Congress.

In 2017, Walberg publicly stated his view on climate change: "I believe that there is a creator in God who is much bigger than us. And I'm confident that, if there's a real problem, He can take care of it." That quote was reported in multiple news outlets at the time, including a CNN clip that circulated widely.

## What This Costs Constituents

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which Walberg voted for, includes provisions that directly affect energy policy. The law eliminates the $7,500 federal tax credit for new electric vehicles and reverts royalty rates for oil and gas extraction on federal public lands to pre-Inflation Reduction Act levels. It also mandates oil and gas lease sales on federal lands across multiple states and restores non-competitive leasing that allows companies to purchase unsold parcels for $1.50 per acre.

Federal royalty revenue from public land extraction flows to the U.S. Treasury and to state governments. Lower royalty rates reduce the public's return on resources extracted from publicly owned lands.

Michigan's automotive industry has made significant investments in electric vehicle manufacturing. The repeal of the EV tax credit removes a consumer purchasing incentive for electric vehicles, including those manufactured at facilities in Michigan.

The STOCK Act's transparency requirements exist precisely to give the public the ability to identify whether lawmakers' financial holdings overlap with their policy decisions. When those disclosures arrive more than a year late, that oversight function is undermined for the period in which the undisclosed holdings existed.
